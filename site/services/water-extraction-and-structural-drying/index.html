The two most important hours in any water loss are the first two. Water extraction is the rapid removal of standing water and water held in carpet, pad and porous surfaces; structural drying is the controlled, measured process that follows to bring every affected material back to its normal moisture content. Together they are the core of water damage mitigation, and the speed and quality of this work determine whether a loss ends in a few days of drying or weeks of demolition and mold remediation.

Why extraction speed matters

Removing water as liquid is hundreds of times more efficient than evaporating it. Every gallon extracted by a pump or wand is a gallon that dehumidifiers do not have to pull from the air later. Fast extraction also limits how far water wicks into drywall and framing, keeps carpet from delaminating, prevents hardwood from cupping and shortens the window in which mold can take hold.

Restoration crews use truck-mounted extractors with high lift and airflow for carpet and pad, weighted ride-on extractors that compress carpet to squeeze out water, portable extractors for upper floors and submersible pumps for deep water.

The science of structural drying

Drying follows a simple principle: water moves from wet materials to drier air, and that air must then be dehumidified or exhausted so it can absorb more. Technicians balance four factors to speed this up: temperature (warmer air holds more moisture), humidity (drier air pulls harder), airflow (moving air lifts moisture off surfaces) and time. Equipment is chosen and placed according to formulas in the IICRC S500 standard based on the volume of wet material and its porosity.

  • Air movers (centrifugal and axial fans) create high-velocity airflow across wet surfaces
  • Low-grain refrigerant (LGR) dehumidifiers remove moisture efficiently in typical indoor conditions
  • Desiccant dehumidifiers work in cold spaces and achieve very low humidity for dense materials
  • Injection and cavity drying systems push warm, dry air inside walls, under cabinets and beneath floors
  • Heat drying systems accelerate evaporation in large or saturated structures

Moisture detection and monitoring

You cannot dry what you cannot measure. Crews use pin and pinless moisture meters, thermo-hygrometers and infrared cameras to map the extent of the water on day one and establish a dry standard from unaffected materials in the same building. Readings are taken daily and logged. Equipment is adjusted as materials dry and removed only when every monitored point reaches the dry standard. These logs are also the proof your insurance carrier wants that the structure was dried properly.

Our extraction and drying process

  1. Arrive, assess and stop the source. Safety hazards are addressed, the category of water is determined and the affected area is mapped.
  2. Extract. Standing water and water held in carpet and pad are removed with the appropriate extraction equipment.
  3. Remove what cannot be dried. Wet pad, swollen trim and saturated or contaminated drywall and insulation are removed so air can reach framing.
  4. Set drying equipment. Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed according to the volume and type of wet material; cavities are opened or injected as needed.
  5. Monitor daily. Moisture readings and humidity are logged every day and equipment is repositioned as the structure dries.
  6. Verify dry and remove equipment. When all materials meet the dry standard, the equipment comes out and a final report is produced.

Drying specific materials

Hardwood floors can often be saved with floor-drying mats that pull moisture up through the boards, but only if drying starts within a day or two. Tile over a wet subfloor usually requires drying from below or removal. Plaster and concrete dry slowly and need patient, well-documented drying. Cabinets are dried by removing toe kicks and injecting air behind them. Crawlspaces require the removal of wet insulation and vapor barriers and the use of dehumidifiers rather than simply fans, which can pull moist outdoor air in.

What it costs and what insurance covers

Extraction and drying are billed by the equipment used per day, the labor involved and any materials removed. The total depends on the number of rooms, the volume of wet material and how many days the drying takes. Because mitigation prevents further damage, insurance carriers generally cover it for any covered loss and expect it to begin promptly. Most restoration companies bill covered mitigation directly to the carrier and provide the moisture logs that support the invoice.

Water Extraction & Drying FAQs

Can I dry water damage with household fans?

For a small spill on a hard surface, yes. For anything that reached carpet pad, drywall or subfloor, household fans move air but do not remove moisture from it, so the water simply redistributes. Professional drying pairs airflow with dehumidification and verifies results with meters.

How many days does structural drying take?

Typically three to five days for most residential losses. Dense materials, cold conditions, contaminated water and delayed starts all extend drying time.

Why is the drying equipment so loud and warm?

Air movers run at high speed to lift moisture off surfaces, and dehumidifiers release warm, dry air as they operate. Both are necessary for efficient drying. Turning equipment off at night significantly extends the drying time and the cost.

Do you have to cut the walls open?

Not always. If drywall is intact, the water was clean and drying starts quickly, walls can often be dried by removing baseboard and drilling small holes at the bottom for airflow. Sagging, swollen or contaminated drywall is removed.

How do you know when it is dry?

Moisture meters compare affected materials to a dry standard measured in unaffected areas of the same building. Equipment is removed when all monitored points reach that standard, not when surfaces merely feel dry.
